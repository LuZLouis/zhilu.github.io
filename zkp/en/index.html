Zero-Knowledge Proofs, Illustrated

How can you convince someone that a claim is true without letting them learn why it is true? These notes start from an ordinary hotel check-in and, through figures and small examples you can work out by hand, introduce in turn the basic concepts of zero-knowledge proofs, the first practical protocol, and the way to remove interaction.

Prerequisites: discrete mathematics, elementary probabilityExamples: a small group modulo 23, all computable by handAdapted from Zero-Knowledge Proofs: Protocols, Constructions, and Applications

Statements and Witnesses

KEY POINTS

  • Distinguish the statement from the witness
  • Use a relation R(x,w) to capture “the witness is valid”
  • A zero-knowledge proof conveys only the conclusion “a witness exists”

Late at night, a traveller checks in at a hotel front desk. The desk needs to confirm just one thing: the guest is at least eighteen years old. Yet the moment the guest hands over an ID card, the clerk sees everything on it: name, photo, date of birth, ID number, and home address.

There are three parties here. The guest must show that they are eligible to check in; the front desk must make a decision on the spot; and the police registration system needs the ability to trace the stay after the fact. The last two needs differ: the desk only needs a conclusion, while the police only need the information to be retrievable when required. A physical ID card cannot separate the two, so “being seen on the spot” and “being traceable later” are bundled together.

This example reveals a general phenomenon: verifying that something is true and understanding why it is true are, in principle, two separable things. To make this precise, we first need to distinguish two objects.

Definition 1.1Statement, witness and relation

Let R be an efficiently computable binary relation. For a public statement x, if R(x,w)=1, we call w a witness for x. The prover wants to convince the verifier that

∃w: R(x,w)=1,

without revealing w itself.

Many seemingly unrelated scenarios share this structure; see Table 1.1.

Table 1.1 Examples of statements, witnesses and relations

Statement xWitness wMeaning of R(x,w)=1
The guest is at least 18Date of birthAt least 18 years lie between the date of birth and today
This sudoku has a solutionThe filled-in gridThe grid obeys the rules and agrees with the puzzle
Graph G has a Hamiltonian cycleA cycle CC visits every vertex of G exactly once
Knowing the discrete logarithm of yAn integer wgw=y

Simply showing the witness would of course convince the verifier, but the verifier would also obtain the witness itself. What a zero-knowledge proof aims to do is convey only the conclusion “such a w exists”.

Note “A witness exists” and “the prover knows a witness” are two different requirements. For discrete logarithms, the discrete logarithm of y always exists, so the meaningful statement is “the prover knows it”. This distinction will surface in Lecture 6 through special soundness.

Three Basic Properties

KEY POINTS

  • Completeness and soundness constrain the verifier’s decision
  • Zero-knowledge constrains the verifier’s view
  • The three properties are independent, and all are necessary

A proof system consists of a prover P and a verifier V; at the end, V outputs “accept” or “reject”. Three basic requirements are placed on such a system.

Three basic propertiesInformal

Completeness: if the statement is true and P holds a witness, then when the protocol is executed honestly, V accepts.

Soundness: if the statement is false, then whatever strategy P uses, the probability that V accepts is small.

Zero-knowledge: everything V sees during the interaction contains no information beyond the fact that the statement is true.

The first two properties constrain V’s decision and can be understood via the four outcomes in Figure 2.1: completeness bounds the probability that a true statement is rejected, and soundness bounds the probability that a false statement is accepted.

The third property constrains V’s view, namely all the messages V receives during the interaction together with its own randomness. The three properties are independent: each of the three verification mechanisms in Table 2.1 violates exactly one of them.

Table 2.1 Verification mechanisms that each violate one property

Verification mechanismCompletenessSoundnessZero-knowledge
Accept regardless of inputHoldsViolatedHolds
Reject regardless of inputViolatedHoldsHolds
Read the full date of birth, then decideHoldsHoldsViolated

The third row is exactly the ID-card approach from Lecture 1: the decision is entirely correct, but the view contains information irrelevant to the decision. As a research area, the difficulty of zero-knowledge proofs lies not in “how to verify” but in how to verify without leaking the witness.

History These three properties were formulated by Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff in 1985, in the paper that introduced interactive proof systems. They measured the extra information leaked by a proof via its “knowledge complexity”; zero-knowledge is the case where the knowledge complexity is zero. The three received the 2012 Turing Award for this line of work.

LECTURE 3Chapter 2 of the book

Interaction and Soundness Error

KEY POINTS

  • A single-round protocol has soundness error 1/2
  • After t independent repetitions it drops to 2−t
  • The three-step commit–challenge–response structure

This lecture uses a concrete protocol to show what interaction buys us. Let the public graph G have n vertices, and suppose the prover knows a Hamiltonian cycle C of G, i.e. a cycle that visits every vertex exactly once. The prover wants to convince the verifier that G has a Hamiltonian cycle without revealing C.

The key to the protocol is isomorphism: for a permutation π of the vertices, the graph G′=π(G) has the same structure as G, only with the vertices renamed. If C is a Hamiltonian cycle of G, then π(C) is a Hamiltonian cycle of G′.

Protocol 3.1Hamiltonian cycle, single round

  1. P picks a random vertex permutation π, computes G′=π(G), commits to each entry of the adjacency matrix of G′ separately, and sends the commitments.
  2. V picks a random b∈{0,1} and sends it.
  3. If b=0: P opens all the commitments and sends π; V checks that G′=π(G).
    If b=1: P opens only the commitments corresponding to the n edges of π(C); V checks that all opened values are 1 and that these edges form a Hamiltonian cycle.

For now, think of a commitment as a sealed envelope: once sent, its content cannot be changed, and until opened, its content cannot be seen (Lecture 5 gives the precise definition). Figure 3.2 shows what the verifier sees under each of the two challenges.

Completeness is clear: a prover holding C can answer both challenges.

Soundness. Suppose G has no Hamiltonian cycle. Once the commitments are sent, G′ is fixed, and there are only two possibilities: if G′ is isomorphic to G, then G′ has no Hamiltonian cycle either, and the challenge b=1 cannot be answered; otherwise b=0 cannot be answered. As Figure 3.3 shows, a dishonest prover P∗ can answer at most one of the two challenges.

Hence in the single-round protocol, P∗ succeeds with probability at most 1/2. This upper bound is called the soundness error of the protocol. As long as we run only one round, it is a fixed positive number.

Independent repetition

Repeat the protocol independently for t rounds, choosing fresh π and b in each round. P∗ must meet a challenge it can answer in every single round, so

εs​=(21​)t=2−t.

Table 3.1 Number of rounds and soundness error

Rounds t110204080
εs​1/2≈10−3≈10−6≈10−12≈10−24

A quantity that tends to zero faster than the inverse of any polynomial as the parameter grows is called negligible. 2−t is a typical example: it is always greater than zero, yet it can be pushed down to a level that need not be considered in practice.

Note Each round consists of three steps: P first sends a commitment, V then sends a random challenge, and P responds. The challenge must be generated after the commitment and must be unpredictable; otherwise P∗ could prepare G′ for that particular challenge, and the argument above would immediately break down. This “commit–challenge–response” structure runs through all of the following lectures. The analysis of repetition above assumes ideal envelopes; with computationally binding commitments, analysing parallel repetition takes more work (Chapters 5 and 7 of the book).

LECTURE 4Chapter 2 of the book

Interactive Proof Systems

KEY POINTS

  • Transcripts and views
  • The definition of an interactive proof system
  • Proof systems versus argument systems

The analysis in the previous lecture relied on a specific protocol. This lecture gives definitions that do not depend on any particular example.

The prover P and the verifier V are two probabilistic algorithms with a common input, the statement x; in addition, P holds a witness w. The two parties take turns sending messages, and at the end V outputs accept (written 1) or reject (written 0). The sequence of both parties’ messages in one interaction, in order, is called the transcript; V’s transcript together with its own randomness is called V’s view. The most common three-message form is shown in Figure 4.1.

Write ⟨P(w),V⟩(x) for the output of V at the end of the interaction, and let LR​={x:∃w, R(x,w)=1}.

Definition 4.1Interactive proof system

We call (P,V) an interactive proof system for the relation R if V runs in polynomial time and the following hold:

Completeness For every (x,w) with R(x,w)=1,

Pr[⟨P(w),V⟩(x)=1] ≥ 1−εc​;

Soundness For every x∈/LR​ and every prover strategy P∗,

Pr[⟨P∗,V⟩(x)=1] ≤ εs​.

Here εc​ and εs​ are called the completeness error and the soundness error respectively; in general they may be functions of the security parameter λ.

The two inequalities concern different parties: completeness involves only an honest P that follows the protocol, while soundness must hold for every strategy P∗, including provers with unbounded computational power. If soundness holds only against polynomial-time P∗, the system is called an argument system; most of the protocols constructed later from commitments based on computational assumptions fall into this category.

In Protocol 3.1, εc​=0; a single round has εs​=1/2, and after t repetitions εs​=2−t.

The cost of interaction

Lowering εs​ requires more rounds of interaction, and every round requires P and V to be online at the same time and exchange a round trip of messages. In settings such as blockchains, the same proof must be verified independently by a large number of nodes at different times, so real-time interaction is impossible. Beyond the three properties, evaluating a proof system also means considering the number of rounds, the communication cost, and whether the parties must be online simultaneously. The Fiat–Shamir transform of Lecture 8 was proposed precisely to eliminate interaction.

LECTURE 5Chapter 3 of the book

Commitment Schemes

KEY POINTS

  • Hiding and binding
  • Hash-based and Pedersen commitments
  • Fair coin flipping with commitments

Lecture 3 likened a commitment to a sealed envelope. This lecture gives its definition and constructions.

Definition 5.1Commitment scheme

A commitment scheme consists of an algorithm Com. For a message m, the sender picks randomness r, computes C=Com(m;r) and sends it; this is called committing. Later the sender sends (m,r) and the receiver checks that Com(m;r)=C; this is called opening. The scheme should satisfy:

Hiding For any m0​,m1​, the distributions of Com(m0​;r) and Com(m1​;r) are indistinguishable.

Binding It is hard to find m=m′ and r,r′ such that Com(m;r)=Com(m′;r′).

Each property can be perfect (holding against computationally unbounded adversaries) or computational (holding only against polynomial-time adversaries). One can show that no commitment scheme can be both perfectly hiding and perfectly binding: if every C can be opened to only a unique m, then a computationally unbounded adversary can recover m from C.

Hash-based commitments

Let H be a cryptographic hash function and set Com(m;r)=H(m∥r). Binding follows from the collision resistance of H; hiding holds in the random oracle model.

Example 5.1

Take H=SHA-256, m= “heads”, r= 3f9a1c27b4e8d605. Then

C = d330f468 6d639463 f8c7b741 d3cfebf4 …

If the sender later claims m′= “tails”, the receiver computes H(m′∥r)= d6b94e8f… =C, and the opening fails.

Note The randomness r cannot be omitted. If C=H(m) and m takes only the two values “heads” and “tails”, the receiver need only compute two hashes and compare them with C to learn m, and hiding is lost.

Application: coin flipping by telephone

Alice and Bob want to decide the outcome of a coin toss over the telephone. If Bob tosses the coin first and then hears Alice’s guess, Bob can lie about the result; if Alice states her guess first, Bob can choose the outcome accordingly. Blum’s protocol solves this problem with a commitment.

Hiding ensures that Bob does not know m when choosing b; binding ensures that Alice cannot change m after seeing b. As long as one party is honest, the outcome m⊕b is uniformly distributed.

Pedersen commitment Let g,h be two generators of a group of prime order q such that nobody knows logg​h. Set Com(m;r)=gmhr. It is perfectly hiding and computationally binding: finding two different openings is equivalent to computing logg​h. The Pedersen commitment and the Schnorr protocol of the next lecture both belong to the discrete-logarithm family, and it appears again and again in later constructions.

LECTURE 6Chapter 4 of the book

Σ-Protocols: the Schnorr Protocol

KEY POINTS

  • The three messages of the Schnorr protocol
  • Completeness and special soundness
  • Why randomness must never be reused

In this lecture we construct, in a concrete group, a protocol for proving “knowledge of a discrete logarithm”. To keep the arithmetic doable by hand, take p=23 and the subgroup of Z23∗​ generated by g=2, whose order is the prime q=11.

Table 6.1 Powers of g=2 modulo 23

k012345678910
2k mod 23124816918133612

Computing gk from k is easy; conversely, given y, finding k such that gk=y is called the discrete logarithm problem. In this group of only 11 elements a table lookup suffices, but in the groups used in practice (of order about 2256) it is believed to be hard.

The prover holds w and publishes y=gw. The numerical examples in this lecture use w=7, y=27mod23=13.

Protocol 6.1Schnorr protocol

Common input: the group description (p,q,g) and y. Prover’s private input: w with gw=y.

  1. P picks t←$​Zq​ and sends the first message a=gt.
  2. V picks a challenge e←$​Zq​ and sends it.
  3. P sends the response z=t+ewmodq.
  4. V accepts if and only if gz=a⋅ye.

Example 6.1

P takes t=5 and sends a=25mod23=9. V picks e=4. P computes z=5+4⋅7mod11=0. Verification:

gz=20=1,a⋅ye=9⋅134=9⋅18=162≡1(mod23).

Both sides are equal, so V accepts.

Completeness gz=gt+ew=gt(gw)e=aye.

Special soundness

Suppose that for the same first message a there are two accepting transcripts (a,e,z) and (a,e′,z′) with e=e′. Dividing the two verification equations gives gz−z′=ye−e′, and therefore

w=e−e′z−z′​modq.

This property is called special soundness. It says that a prover able to answer two different challenges for the same a can in fact compute w. This is exactly the precise meaning of “the prover knows w”, and from it one also obtains the soundness error 1/q of a single execution.

Example 6.2

Keep t=5, a=9 from Example 6.1. If the prover responds once more, to the challenge e′=2, with z′=5+2⋅7mod11=8, then

w=(0−8)⋅(4−2)−1≡3⋅6=18≡7(mod11).

Note Example 6.2 also shows that, in implementations, the randomness t must never be reused. A game-console signing-key leak disclosed in 2010 was caused precisely by the signature algorithm (ECDSA) using the same random number in different signatures.

The Schnorr protocol has the three-message “first message–challenge–response” form and satisfies completeness, special soundness, and the zero-knowledge property discussed in the next lecture. Three-message protocols with these properties are collectively called Σ-protocols.

LECTURE 7Chapter 5 of the book

Simulators and Zero-Knowledge

KEY POINTS

  • The idea of a simulator
  • The definition of honest-verifier zero-knowledge
  • A simulator for the Schnorr protocol

In the Schnorr protocol the verifier receives three numbers, (a,e,z). How can we argue that it learns nothing about w from them?

The idea is to argue the other way round: if an algorithm that does not hold w can also generate transcripts distributed identically to real interactions, then such a transcript contains no information that only w could have provided. Such an algorithm is called a simulator.

Definition 7.1Honest-verifier zero-knowledge

A protocol is honest-verifier zero-knowledge if there exists a polynomial-time algorithm S such that for every (x,w) with R(x,w)=1, the output of S(x) is identically distributed (perfect) or computationally indistinguishable (computational) from the view of the honest V in a real interaction.

For the Schnorr protocol, the simulator first chooses the challenge and the response, and then solves for the first message:

e←$​Zq​,z←$​Zq​,a=gz⋅y−e.

The resulting (a,e,z) automatically satisfies gz=aye.

Example 7.1

Take e=3, z=6. Then gz=26≡18, y3=133≡12, 12−1≡2(mod23), so a=18⋅2=36≡13. Check: a⋅ye=13⋅12=156≡18=gz, so the transcript (13,3,6) is an accepting transcript, and w was never used to generate it.

Proposition 7.1

The Schnorr protocol is perfect honest-verifier zero-knowledge.

Proof In a real interaction, e is uniform and independent of t; for fixed e, the map t↦z=t+ew is a bijection on Zq​, so z is uniform and independent of e; and a=gt=gzy−e is uniquely determined by (e,z). In the simulator’s output, (e,z) is likewise uniform on Zq​×Zq​, and a is determined by the same formula. The two distributions are identical. □

Seen from another angle: there are exactly q2 transcripts satisfying the verification equation, and each (e,z) corresponds to a unique a. Both the real interaction and the simulator are uniform over these q2 transcripts. Figure 7.2 lists all accepting transcripts for y=13: for each challenge e, the 11 responses z correspond exactly to 11 distinct first messages a.

Note The simulator succeeds because it can choose e first. In a real interaction, e is chosen by V only after a has been sent, so the prover has no such freedom. This also explains the requirement from Lecture 3 that “the challenge must come after the commitment”.

Definition 7.1 considers only verifiers that choose the challenge at random as the protocol prescribes. A malicious verifier may choose e depending on a; simulating it requires techniques such as rewinding; see Chapter 5 of the book.

LECTURE 8Chapter 6 of the book

The Fiat–Shamir Transform

KEY POINTS

  • Replacing the verifier with a hash function
  • Non-interactive proofs and Schnorr signatures
  • What must go into the hash

Lecture 4 pointed out that interaction requires the prover and the verifier to be online at the same time. In public-coin protocols such as the Schnorr protocol, the verifier’s only role is to supply an unpredictable challenge. In 1986, Fiat and Shamir proposed replacing the verifier with a hash function that generates the challenge.

Construction 8.1Non-interactive Schnorr proof

  1. P picks t←$​Zq​ and computes a=gt.
  2. P computes e=H(g,y,a,m)modq, where m is an accompanying message.
  3. P computes z=t+ewmodq and outputs the proof π=(a,z).
  4. Any verifier recomputes e and checks that gz=a⋅ye.

Because e depends on a, the prover learns the challenge only after fixing a, and cannot, like the simulator, choose e first and then solve for a. When m is a message to be signed, π is precisely the Schnorr signature on that message.

Example 8.1

Keep w=7, y=13, t=5, a=9. If H(2,13,9,m)mod11=4, then z=0, and the proof is π=(9,0). The verifier recomputes e=4, and verification proceeds exactly as in Example 6.1.

What should go into the hash

The input to the hash must include the first message a and the public statement. Suppose an implementation mistakenly uses e=H(m)modq. Then e can be computed before the proof is produced, and an attacker can forge a proof without w:

Example 8.2Forgery under weak Fiat–Shamir

Suppose H(m)mod11=3. Proceeding like the simulator, the attacker takes z=6, computes a=g6y−3=13 (see Example 7.1), and outputs π=(13,6). The verifier computes e=H(m)mod11=3, checks g6=18=13⋅133, and accepts.

This flaw has appeared repeatedly in real-world systems and is known as the “weak Fiat–Shamir” problem. The principle is: everything the verifier would have seen first in the interaction (the public statement, the public parameters, and all previous messages) must be fed into the hash.

Note The security of Construction 8.1 is proved in the random oracle model, i.e. by treating H as a truly random function. In this model the simulator can itself program the value of the hash at (g,y,a,m) and thus reuse the simulation method of Lecture 7; knowledge extraction, on the other hand, requires the forking lemma. See Chapter 6 of the book for details.

Epilogue: From Foundations to Applications

KEY POINTS

  • The two main threads of the book
  • Proofs of computation
  • Privacy-preserving authentication

The preceding eight lectures cover the basic framework of zero-knowledge proofs: statements and witnesses; completeness, soundness and zero-knowledge; the commit–challenge–response structure; commitment schemes; Σ-protocols and special soundness; simulators; and the Fiat–Shamir transform. The later chapters of the book build on these foundations along two main threads.

Proofs of computation. When a computation is outsourced to someone else, the verifier wants to confirm that the result is correct without redoing the computation. This requires expressing arbitrary computations as algebraic constraints (arithmetization) and using tools such as polynomial commitments and sum-check to build succinct proofs, whose length and verification time are far smaller than the computation itself. Chapters 9 and 10 of the book discuss constructions such as Groth16, PLONK and FRI; Chapter 13 covers recursive proofs and folding; and Chapter 15 covers the system design of verifiable computation.

Privacy-preserving authentication. Back to the check-in scenario of Lecture 1: an issuer issues a digital credential, and each time the holder presents it, they prove only the attributes required (for example, “at least 18 years old”), with different presentations remaining unlinkable. Chapter 11 of the book discusses range proofs, and Chapters 16 to 18 cover, in turn, the definition of credentials, issuance and presentation under signatures such as BBS+, and revocation.

In addition, Chapter 12 introduces the MPC-in-the-head construction based on multiparty computation, Chapter 14 discusses the post-quantum setting, and Chapter 19 reviews the whole book and surveys frontier directions.
